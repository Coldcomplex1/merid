ARTICLE:
## Which tools should a Vietnamese speaker actually use to learn English?

Use five, one per skill. Merid for vocabulary while you read, ELSA Speak for
pronunciation, Anki for remembering what you meet, Language Reactor for
listening, and Cambridge Dictionary with YouGlish for checking a word properly.
They do different jobs, so they stack rather than compete.

I should say the awkward thing first. I built Merid. So read the order below as
the sequence I would install them in, not as a claim that a reading tool beats a
speaking tool. Those two are not even measured on the same scale.

The list is short on purpose. I have downloaded and quit more vocabulary apps
than I can name, always for the same reason: each one asked for a separate block
of time that my day did not have. So the question I used to pick these was not
"which is most powerful", it was "which one survives a bad week".

| Tool | The one job it does | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Merid | Vocabulary, while you read Vietnamese pages | Free |
| ELSA Speak | Pronunciation, with feedback on your own voice | Free tier, paid subscription |
| Anki | Keeping what you already met | Free except on iPhone |
| Language Reactor | Listening, with dual subtitles | Free tier, paid upgrade |
| Cambridge + YouGlish | Checking a single word properly | Free |

## Why is a tool I built myself sitting at number one?

Because it is the only one here that asks for no extra time. Merid is a free
Chrome extension for Vietnamese speakers learning English. While you read
ordinary Vietnamese pages, VnExpress, Tuổi Trẻ, Vietnamese Wikipedia, Facebook,
it swaps a small number of Vietnamese words for their English equivalents. The
reading you already do becomes the practice.

That is the whole design. Nothing to open, no streak to protect, no session to
schedule. You keep reading the news at breakfast and a few words are in English
now.

Some specifics, so this is checkable rather than a pitch. Word replacement runs
locally on your own machine, so the pages you read are not sent anywhere. There
is an optional AI context check that verifies a substituted word genuinely fits
its sentence, because a dictionary-correct word in the wrong context teaches you
the wrong thing. It ships three word lists: SAT (988 words), C1 (1,379) and C2
(977). Words you save go to a deck you can review later.

![A Merid deck with twenty five saved words, fifteen still being learned and ten marked as known](/blog/screenshots/my-deck.png)

Now the part that matters more. **Merid teaches reading vocabulary and nothing
else.** It will not improve your pronunciation, it will not help you hear fast
speech, it will not teach grammar, and it will not make you able to hold a
conversation. Every other tool on this list beats it at the thing that tool
does. That is exactly why the list has five entries and not one.

## How do I stop forgetting a word a week after I meet it?

Anki. It is a flashcard program built on spaced repetition, which means it shows
you a card just before you were about to forget it and stretches the gap each
time you get it right. Meeting a word once teaches you almost nothing. Meeting
it on the right days is what makes it stick.

Anki is free on Windows, macOS, Linux and Android, and free to sync through
AnkiWeb. On iPhone the official app is a one-off purchase, and Anki's own FAQ
explains that the iOS sales are what funds development of everything else.
Check the current price on [apps.ankiweb.net](https://apps.ankiweb.net/) rather
than trusting a number in a blog post.

## What actually fixes Vietnamese-accented English?

ELSA Speak, because it listens to you rather than to a recording. You speak into
it and it scores your pronunciation and tells you which sounds were wrong, down
to the syllable. Reading about the difference between two English sounds does not
transfer. Being told you just got one wrong, twenty times, does.

ELSA was founded in 2015 by Vu Van, who grew up in Hanoi, and the app is built
around exactly the problem of a non-native speaker who is fluent on paper and
hard to follow out loud. There is a free tier and a paid subscription. Prices
and plans are on [elsaspeak.com](https://elsaspeak.com/).

What it does not do is teach you words. It sharpens the ones you have.

## How do I train listening without quitting in a week?

Language Reactor, on things you were going to watch anyway. It is a browser
extension that puts two sets of subtitles on Netflix and YouTube at once, so you
get the English line and a translation together, and you can click any word to
see what it means without pausing to open a dictionary.

The reason this works is the same reason Merid works. It attaches learning to an
activity you already do rather than asking for a new one. The free version covers
dual subtitles on both sites; the more advanced features are paid. Their own site
is [languagereactor.com](https://www.languagereactor.com/).

Set the second subtitle to Vietnamese at first, then drop it when you notice you
have stopped reading it.

## Where should I check what a word really means?

Cambridge Dictionary for the meaning, YouGlish for the sound.
[Cambridge](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/) gives you a definition written in
simple English, example sentences, and audio in both British and American
accents. It is free and it does not require an account.

[YouGlish](https://youglish.com/) is the one people do not know about, and it is
the better half of this pair. You type a word and it finds real YouTube videos of
people saying it, starting playback at the exact sentence. So instead of one
studio recording of a word, you hear fifteen ordinary speakers using it at normal
speed with normal accents.

Use it whenever a word feels slippery. Hearing it five times in five real
sentences settles it faster than reading the phonetic spelling ever will.

## Which one should I start with this week?

One. Not five.

If you read Vietnamese news or Facebook daily, install Merid and change nothing
else about your week. If people ask you to repeat yourself when you speak
English, start with ELSA instead. If your problem is that you learn words and
lose them, your problem is Anki-shaped and no amount of new input will fix it.

Add the second tool only after the first has survived a week where you were busy
and tired. That week is the real test, and it is the one every study app I ever
deleted failed.
