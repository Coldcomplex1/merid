ARTICLE:
This question usually gets answered with a round number. Three thousand words.
Five thousand. I think the number is the least interesting part of it, so this
post is about the part that actually decides whether you can read the article.

## So how many words do you need?

It is a ratio problem, not a total. What determines whether you read fluently is
how often you hit a word you do not know, not how many you know altogether.

Run the arithmetic. Knowing ninety percent of the words on a page sounds
excellent, but it means one unknown word in every ten. A news sentence runs past
twenty words, so you stall roughly twice per sentence. That is not reading, that
is translating.

Ninety-five percent gets you to one in twenty, about one stall per sentence.
Still exhausting. Around ninety-eight percent you are down to one unknown word in
fifty, so every two or three sentences, and that is the point where you can guess
from context instead of stopping.

## Why is ninety-five to ninety-eight the hard part?

Because the words in that gap are not common ones. The first few thousand words
turn up everywhere, so you absorb them almost automatically. The further up you
go, the rarer each new word is, and the fewer chances it gets to repeat often
enough to stick.

This is precisely where list-based study runs out. You can cram three hundred
words in a fortnight, but if you never meet them in a real sentence afterwards,
most will drain away. Not because you were lazy. Because your brain drops what it
does not use.

That is also where I gave up on the vocabulary apps I had installed. I would
learn a few hundred words, come back three weeks later, and be starting over.

## How do you get enough repetitions, then?

By letting new words appear inside the things you already read every day. If you
are Vietnamese you are already reading VnExpress, Vietnamese Wikipedia and
Facebook. The volume is not the problem. The problem is that none of those words
are in English.

Merid inserts them. It swaps a small number of Vietnamese words on the page in
front of you for their English equivalents, so new vocabulary arrives inside a
real sentence rather than on a flashcard.

![A Vietnamese Wikipedia article with the word kết hợp replaced by amalgamate](/blog/screenshots/wikipedia-amalgamate.png)

Nothing extra opens. No study session appears in your calendar. The words come to
where you already are, while you are reading something you actually care about.

## How should you measure progress without fooling yourself?

Count words you have marked as known, not minutes studied. Minutes tell you
nothing. Words you recognise on sight the next time they appear tell you
something.

![The deck page showing 25 words, 15 learning and 10 known](/blog/screenshots/my-deck.png)

That is my own deck at the time of the screenshot. Twenty-five words, fifteen in
progress, ten known. It contains *hardy*, *innovate*, *copious*, *amalgamate* and
*confluence*.

I never sat down and studied any of them. They showed up while I was reading, I
kept them, and by the third encounter I marked them known. Twenty-five is a small
number. It is also twenty-five words that cost me no separate study time at all.

## What is the number, then?

The rhythm matters more than the total. Meet a few new words in real sentences
every day, keep that up over months, and you will cross ninety-eight percent
coverage without ever having counted.

If you want a target anyway, set it weekly rather than cumulatively. Choose one
list, keep the swap intensity low, and read the way you were going to read
regardless.
